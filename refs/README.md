## Summaries

One entry per file, alphabetical by BibTeX key. Each summary was written from the file's own text (abstract, introduction, results and conclusion; for the two long documents, BPMN 2.0 and the self-driving-laboratory review, the front matter and conclusions). The last line of each entry says where and why the report cites it.

### `abbott2015programs.pdf` — abbott2015programs
**Abbott, K., Bogart, C. and Walkingshaw, E. (2015). Programs for people: What we can learn from lab protocols. IEEE Symposium on Visual Languages and Human-Centric Computing (VL/HCC), pp. 203–211.**  
The paper asks what language features a mixed-initiative programming environment needs when a human, not a computer, executes the program, and studies a formalized class of such "programs": 20 peer-reviewed protocols from Cold Spring Harbor Protocols, coded step by step (88% inter-rater agreement on a subset). It finds that protocols present a linear, idealized process while assuming imprecise execution, and identifies six features that widen or narrow the "path" the executor follows: optionality and wiggle room (broadening; in 50% and 70% of protocols) versus advice, constraints and expected outcomes (narrowing; advice and constraints appear in 95–100% of protocols and account for about 13% of instructions each), plus contingencies. Contrary to Licklider's claim that instructions to people specify goals rather than courses, many steps are straightforward imperative instructions. The authors conclude that protocols supply a toolbox for specifying tasks for humans (explicit limits and liberties, a simplified linear structure, stated intermediate results in lieu of error handling) that should inform mixed-initiative DSLs and workflow tools.  
*In the report:* Cited in the introduction for the phrase "programs for people" — recipes, protocols and run sheets written by one person for another that present an idealized linear process and rely on conventions for widening or narrowing what a step means.

### `allen1983intervals.pdf` — allen1983intervals
**Allen, J. F. (1983). Maintaining knowledge about temporal intervals. Communications of the ACM, 26(11), 832–843.**  
Allen addresses representing temporal knowledge that is relative and imprecise (no absolute dates), arguing for intervals rather than points as the primitive. He defines the thirteen mutually exclusive relations that can hold between two intervals (before, meets, overlaps, during, starts, finishes, equals and their inverses) and stores them as disjunctive labels on arcs of a network, with a constraint-propagation algorithm using a transitivity table to tighten labels as facts are added. Reference intervals impose a hierarchy that bounds the propagation work predictably. Examples cover historical databases, process modeling and a continually updated "now"; the paper also sketches extensions for durations, dates and persistence, with the duration reasoner implemented.  
*In the report:* Cited as the source of the qualitative interval relations underlying the trigger vocabulary, and as the grounding for the OWL-Time `$comment` annotations in the schemas (e.g. `afterStep` with zero offset as `intervalMetBy`).

### `almuntashiri2025provenance.pdf` — almuntashiri2025provenance
**Almuntashiri, A. H., Ibáñez, L.-D. and Chapman, A. (2025). Using LLMs to infer provenance information. Proceedings of ProvenanceWeek (PW '25), Berlin, Germany. ACM, 10 pp.**  
Notes that provenance capture requires either provenance-enabled systems, which remain the exception, or human documentation, so the standard place for the provenance of a scientific experiment is still the paper, where it is buried in text. The authors build an LLM-based extractor, identify the most effective prompt for provenance extraction, and report that ChatGPT-4o with a few-shot prompt extracts provenance information from biomedical publications with an average accuracy, precision and recall of 73% in the extraction experiment, and precision and recall above 90% in a user study. The extractor was scaled to all exome-sequencing experiment papers in PubMed; the authors list prompt refinement and dropping non-essential text such as references as ways to cut its time and resource cost.  
*In the report:* Cited in the importers paragraph as evidence that a language model is a stronger extractor of structured process descriptions from prose than Rhylthyme's structure-based importers, the same move an agent makes when it reads a recipe or protocol and authors a program through the MCP server.

### `angelopoulos2026prompts.pdf` — angelopoulos2026prompts
**Angelopoulos, A., Cahoon, J. F. and Alterovitz, R. (2026). From prompts to protocols: An AI agent for laboratory automation. arXiv:2605.16552.**  
The paper targets the barrier that lab orchestration software poses to scientists (code, configuration files, scheduling semantics) by adding an LLM agent to the Experiment Orchestration System (EOS). The agent runs an agentic loop over an MCP server exposing over 40 EOS tools: it reads task and device specifications, generates a protocol as a DAG of tasks with parameters, validates it through EOS's validation engine via MCP, and corrects errors automatically; mutating operations require user approval. A visual graph editor stays synchronized with the agent's protocol representation so users can alternate between natural-language and manual editing, and the agent also runs and monitors closed-loop optimization campaigns and analyzes results. On three simulated labs (color mixing, solubility/purification, liquid-liquid extraction) using Claude Sonnet 4.6, the agent achieved a 97% first-attempt success rate across 65 trials and roughly an order-of-magnitude reduction in interface actions.  
*In the report:* Cited three times — as evidence that LLM agents now draft formal protocols from prose, as one of the two nearest laboratory neighbours to Rhylthyme's authoring path (noting it targets robotic execution), and as a parallel to Rhylthyme's validate-and-correct MCP design.

### `anthropic2024mcp.pdf` — anthropic2024mcp
**Anthropic (2024). Introducing the Model Context Protocol. Announcement, 25 November 2024 (spec revision 2024-11-05).**  
This is the web announcement (captured 2026-09-09) open-sourcing MCP as a standard for connecting AI assistants to data sources and tools, motivated by the cost of bespoke integrations for every data source. It describes the architecture — developers expose data through MCP servers or build MCP clients — and the three released components: the specification and SDKs, local server support in Claude Desktop, and an open repository of pre-built servers (Google Drive, Slack, GitHub, Postgres, Puppeteer). It names early adopters (Block, Apollo, Zed, Replit, Codeium, Sourcegraph) and credits David Soria Parra and Justin Spahr-Summers as creators. It is an announcement rather than a technical document; protocol details live in the specification.  
*In the report:* Cited as the primary reference for MCP when introducing the server ("standardizes how a language-model host discovers and calls tools, reads resources and invokes prompts"), with `mcpspec2025` covering the later transport and structured-output revisions.

### `autoprotocol.pdf` — autoprotocol
**Strateos (2023). Autoprotocol specification. autoprotocol.org.**  
The file is a one-page capture of the autoprotocol.org home page. It describes Autoprotocol as a language for specifying experimental protocols precisely and unambiguously for both humans and machines, originally developed at Transcriptic (now Strateos) to define experiments run remotely on robotic automation, and since grown into an open standard with a community and an autoprotocol-python library for generating protocols. The page links to the specification and the Autoprotocol Standard Changes process but contains no technical content itself (the JSON instruction format, container/reagent model and so on are only in the linked spec).  
*In the report:* Cited as the machine-readable protocol layer ("Autoprotocol's JSON protocol description") in the layered lab-software stack between device drivers and human-readable protocol platforms.

### `badosa2019resource.pdf` — badosa2019resource
**Badosa, F., Espinosa, A., Acevedo, C., Vera, G. and Ripoll, A. (2019). A history-based resource manager for genome analysis workflows applications on clusters with heterogeneous nodes. International Journal of Parallel Programming 47(2):317–342. Published online 2018.**  
Observes that the resource usage of a bioinformatics application varies substantially between executions and that cluster resource managers ignore this, wasting capacity. Proposes a History-Based Resource Manager with a prediction model that generates multiple performance predictions per job under different combinations of cluster resources, and a scheduling algorithm that considers each node's degree of multiprogramming and co-schedules applications on the same node according to their compatibility. Evaluated on a real cluster with two workloads (4 workflows and 30 applications; 6 workflows and 52 applications) and in the WorkflowSim simulator, it improves on SLURM with first-come-first-served by about 32% in average makespan, 79% in efficiency and 99% in resource usage, and on Max–Min and Min–Min scheduling by about 31% in makespan.  
*In the report:* Cited in Limitations and future work: its history-based duration prediction suggests calibrating `defaultSeconds` from recorded step end times, and its compatibility-based co-scheduling is the machine counterpart of the fractional `actorsRequired` model.

### `ball2003groundholding.pdf` — ball2003groundholding
**Ball, M. O., Hoffman, R., Odoni, A. R. and Rifkin, R. (2003). A stochastic integer program with dual network structure and its application to the ground-holding problem. Operations Research, 51(1), 167–171.**  
This technical note studies a two-state inventory network-flow model whose deterministic demand is replaced by a discrete set of demand scenarios with probabilities. Although stochastic demand destroys the network structure, the authors show the constraint matrix is dual network (totally unimodular), so the integer program can be solved by LP or network-flow methods. Applied to ground-delay programs, the model chooses a Planned Airport Acceptance Rate vector given a distribution of weather-dependent capacity scenarios, trading ground holding against airborne holding. Its design point is compatibility with Collaborative Decision Making: rather than assigning slots to individual flights (which under CDM emerges from FAA–airline interaction), it decides only the capacity profile, and the authors report work to integrate it into the Flight Schedule Monitor CDM tool.  
*In the report:* Cited as the stochastic ground-holding model designed for CDM integration, in the passage arguing that capacity-as-concurrency-cap and plan-as-shared-artifact carry over from air-traffic flow management to `maxConcurrent` and the shareable timeline.
